# (0) Requirements definition

Requirements definition is the step where the goals and constraints of the AVI system are established. Its objective is to specify the defect catalogue, the tolerances, the required detection and false reject rates, the cycle time and the interfaces to the production line [49]. This step does not appear as a box in the processing chain of Figure 11, due to the fact that it is performed before the lifecycle begins. However, it sets the foundation for all subsequent design choices. For instance, the smallest defect of the catalogue and the field of view define the resolution of step (1), the tolerances define the calibration and the measurement uncertainty of step (2), the cost of each error defines the thresholds of step (6), and the cycle time and the position of the reject station define the allowed latency of step (7). Therefore, a vague requirement (e.g., "detect all relevant scratches") cannot be designed for and cannot be accepted, while a precise one (e.g., "reject scratches longer than 2 mm on the visible face with a detection rate of at least 95%") can be traced through every step and verified at the end. In this section, we describe how the requirements are documented, what they must contain and how they are verified.

## Requirement and system specifications

In machine vision projects, the requirements are typically documented in two documents: requirement and system specification documents. The guideline VDI/VDE/VDMA 2632 Part 2 describes how to prepare both of them [49]. On the one hand, the requirement specification is written by the user of the system, i.e., the manufacturer that operates the line. This document describes what the system must achieve and under which conditions, e.g., the inspection task, the parts and their variants, the defect catalogue, the performance, the environment, the interfaces, the operation and the maintenance. On the other hand, the system specification is written by the vision engineering team. This document describes how the system will meet each requirement, e.g., the cameras, the illumination, the algorithms and the test procedures. In other words, the requirement specification states the problem and the system specification states the proposed solution. Note that the requirement specification should not prescribe a solution (e.g., "use deep learning" or "use a 12-megapixel camera") unless it is a real constraint of the system. 

Each individual requirement must be written so that it can be designed for and verified. The international standard on requirements engineering ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 lists the characteristics of good requirements, e.g., necessary, unambiguous, complete, singular, feasible and verifiable [305]. For AVI, verifiable is the most demanding characteristic, due to the fact that most quality statements are originally formulated for human inspectors. For instance, "no visible scratches" depends on the observer, the light and the viewing distance, while "no scratch longer than 2 mm and wider than 0.1 mm in zone A" can be measured. Additionally, each requirement should be paired with its verification method (i.e., inspection, analysis, demonstration or test) and with an acceptance criteria [305].

The requirements are typically defined by applying the following steps. First, the stakeholders are identified, i.e., quality engineering (owns the defect catalogue and the tolerances), production (owns the line, the cycle time and the space), maintenance, plant IT and, where relevant, the customer and the regulator. Second, samples are collected, i.e., good parts of every variant and defective parts of every defect type. Additionally, the current defect rates and the performance of the current inspection system are also collected. Third, the requirement specification is drafted with the content described in the remainder of this section. Fourth, a feasibility study tests the most critical requirements on the samples, e.g., whether the smallest defect can be made visible with a few candidate illuminations, as discussed in step (1). Finally, the system specification is written, reviewed against every requirement and agreed together with the acceptance test. Note that the collection of defective parts is often the slowest of these steps, due to the fact that defects are rare in a well-running process. Therefore, it should start as early as possible.

## Defect catalogue

The defect catalogue is the central document of the requirements because it defines what the system must find and what it must accept. Each entry of the catalogue typically contains the following elements: (i) metadata including a unique identifier, name of the defect type, a short description, and example images; (ii) a severity class; (iii) the acceptance limits, e.g., maximum length, width, area, depth or contrast, and the maximum number of defects per part or per area; (iv) the limit samples, i.e., physical parts or images just inside and just outside the acceptance limit; and, (v) the evaluation conditions under which the limits apply.

The severity class expresses the consequence of an escape. For instance, for injectable drugs, critical defects make the product unfit for use and may pose a risk to the patient, major defects may impair its function, and minor defects lower its perceived quality without limiting its function [25]. Many appearance specifications define a defect by whether it is visible under given conditions, e.g., at a given viewing distance, illumination and inspection time. These conditions describe a human inspector and not a measurement. Therefore, they must be translated into measurable limits by measuring the limit samples, i.e., size, contrast or height. The limit samples then become the reference for both the inspection system and the annotators of the model sandbox (S).

## Dimensions and tolerances

Dimensional requirements are taken from the technical drawing of the part. Each characteristic is specified with its nominal value, its tolerance and the datums to which it refers [202, 227]. Additionally, the requirement specification states the decision rule for measurements close to a tolerance limit, i.e., whether simple acceptance is used or guard bands reduce the risk of accepting a nonconforming part [199, 203]. Note that the decision rule has a direct cost because every guard band either rejects or sends to review a share of the conforming parts.

The tolerances also define the required quality of the measurement system. A common rule of the Measurement Systems Analysis (MSA) reference manual is that the measurement system should resolve at least one tenth of the tolerance, and that its gauge repeatability and reproducibility should consume less than 10% of the tolerance (values from 10 to 30% may be acceptable depending on the application) [48]. 

## Performance requirements

The performance requirements state how well the system must decide. The performance is measured per part on a test set whose true condition is known. The metrics are: the detection rate, the escape rate and the false reject rate. These rates should be specified per defect type or per severity class rather than as a single average in order to reveal any critical defect type. Additionally, the specification states the maximum Review rate, which must match the capacity of the reviewers. For instance, a Review rate of 2% at 60 parts per minute sends 1.2 parts per minute to a human, which keeps one reviewer busy about 60% of the time when each review takes 30 seconds.

The required rates follow from the costs of the two errors. That is, an escape may cause a complaint, a recall or a safety problem, while a false reject costs the value of a good part. The ratio between both costs defines the optimal thresholds for the vision system [231]. Therefore, the costs should be agreed with production and quality engineering and documented in the requirements.

## Timing requirements

The timing requirements state how fast the system must capture, decide and act. The cycle time (the time available to process one part) follows from the maximum production rate, e.g., 1 s per part at 60 parts per minute. The cycle time should include a margin for speed peaks and for future increases of the line speed. However, the cycle time alone is not sufficient. The line speed and the pixel size define the maximum exposure time of step (1). For instance, at a conveyor speed of 0.2 m/s and a pixel of 0.049 mm, a motion blur below half a pixel requires an exposure shorter than about 120 µs, which calls for strobe lighting. Additionally, the distance from the camera to the reject station and the response time of the actuator define the latency budget of step (7), i.e., the time after the trigger within which the decision must be available. For instance, with the reject station 0.6 m downstream of the camera, the part arrives 3 s after the trigger, so that the decision deadline is 3 s minus the response time of the actuator and a safety margin. Finally, the specification states the start-up time after a power cycle and the maximum time for a product changeover, due to the fact that both affect the availability of the line.

## Interface requirements

The interfaces define how the AVI system is embedded in the line and in the plant, and they are a frequent source of late changes when they are not specified early. Note that the interfaces also include the fail-safe behavior of the system, i.e., what happens when an image is missing, a result arrives late or the system is not ready. The specification should require positive release, i.e., a part is only accepted with an explicit and valid Pass decision.

## Environment, operation, and maintenance

The environmental requirements describe the conditions in which the system must keep its performance, e.g., the range of ambient light, temperature and humidity, vibration, dust, oil and cleaning agents, together with the required IP rating of the housings. These conditions directly influence the enclosure and the mounting of step (1) and the verification intervals of step (2). Additionally, the operational requirements describe who uses the system and how, e.g., the user roles and their training, the procedure for introducing a new variant, the intervals for verification with golden parts, the availability, the spare parts and the support. Finally, the specification states how changes are controlled, i.e., who may change recipes, thresholds and models, how each change is validated in the model sandbox (S) and how it is documented [110]. In regulated industries, these requirements are complemented by the data integrity and validation expectations of the regulators [262, 277].

## Acceptance and verification

The acceptance test closes the requirements definition. Each requirement is verified with the method defined when it was written, and the results are documented in a traceability matrix that links every requirement to its test and its result [305]. For instance, the defect catalogue is verified with a test set that contains good parts, defective parts of every type and the limit samples. The dimensional requirements are verified with calibrated reference parts and a Gage R&R study [48]. The performance requirements are verified with the rates and confidence bounds described above [246, 247]. Finally, the timing and interface requirements are verified with tests at the maximum line speed and under fault conditions, e.g., a missing trigger or a full reject bin.

Note that the acceptance is typically performed twice. First, a factory acceptance test is carried out at the site of the supplier with the test parts. Second, a site acceptance test is carried out on the production line under real conditions, followed by a period of monitored production before the system takes over the decisions. Additionally, the test parts of the acceptance remain in use after commissioning, with the purpose of verifying the system periodically and validating every change in the model sandbox (S).
