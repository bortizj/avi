# Open problems

AVI is a mature technology for well-defined tasks under controlled conditions, e.g., the measurement of a fixtured part under a backlight or the detection of known defects on a stable product, where the methods described in this document reach a high and measurable performance. However, the field still faces several open problems, which appear mostly where natural variability, rare events, and learned models meet the industrial need to prove the performance of a system before and during production. Below we list the most important ones, grouped by the steps of the design process where they arise; note that they also mark the limits of the best practices summarized in the next subsection.

- **Difficult surfaces and the joint design of capture and processing, steps (1) and (3).** Shiny, transparent, and low-texture surfaces remain demanding for 2D and 3D sensing, since glare, multiple reflections, occlusion, and flying pixels create artifacts that either look like defects or hide them [56, 143]. The choice of illumination and spectral band still relies largely on experiments with real good and defective samples [53, 62], while learning the optics together with the processing, e.g., a learned sensor mask with a reconstruction network for time-of-flight cameras [143], promises better data but makes the system harder to interpret and to validate.
- **Consistent data over the life of the system, steps (2) and (3).** Extrinsic calibration can change after a small knock on a camera bracket without any visible sign, and lights change their brightness and color with temperature and age [113]; periodic verifications and reference marks reveal such changes only at intervals or only where the marks are, while learned models can lose performance silently as soon as the images drift away from their training data. Additionally, learned denoisers and enhancers can remove or replace rare structures, i.e., exactly the defects an inspection looks for [128, 130], and there is no established procedure to certify a learned pre-processing beyond test sets and method-noise checks.
- **High-resolution images and small network inputs, steps (3) to (5).** Many anomaly detection models work on inputs of about 224 × 224 pixels, while inspection images have several megapixels; therefore, resizing loses small defects and tiling multiplies the computation within a fixed cycle time [140, 141].
- **Robustness of learned models to new conditions, steps (4) and (5).** A learned detector is only valid for the conditions represented in its training data, and a new background, product, or supplier can degrade it without any visible error (Figure 25), while detecting such a domain shift reliably at run time remains difficult [275]. Alongside it, pose estimation of parts that are known only from their CAD model has improved strongly [159], but reflective surfaces, little texture, and symmetries remain demanding cases [183], and the behavior of promptable segmentation and open-vocabulary detection models on a specific product cannot be guaranteed [177, 178], which currently limits them mainly to labeling and prototyping.
- **From anomalies to defects, step (5).** Anomaly detectors flag deviations from good parts without judging whether a deviation is critical, and acceptable variation, e.g., of natural products or textures, overlaps with faint defects (Figure 29); linking anomaly scores to the defect catalogue and its limit samples remains largely manual [197, 198]. Additionally, logical anomalies, e.g., a missing or misplaced component, require an understanding of the whole image [218], and vision-language models and synthetic defects reduce the need for real defect images [212, 220, 221, 224], but how to prove their detection rate for a specific product without many real defective parts is still open.
- **Realistic benchmarks and operating thresholds, steps (5) and (6).** Methods exceed 99% AUROC on MVTec AD [216], while state-of-the-art methods remain below 60% average AU-PRO on MVTec AD 2, which includes transparent objects, varying lighting, and very small defects [219]. Moreover, production runs at one threshold, whereas benchmarks report threshold-independent metrics that do not always predict threshold-dependent results [219]; estimating a low false reject rate reliably needs many good images, and the scores drift with the process.
- **Alignment-dependent 3D results, steps (4) and (5).** CAD comparisons depend on the alignment, i.e., the same scan can pass with a best-fit alignment and fail with a datum alignment (Figure 30), and the automatic alignment of nearly flat or symmetric parts can stop in local minima [149, 227].
- **Calibrated decisions and correlated evidence, step (6).** Cost-based thresholds and Review zones need calibrated probabilities, but modern networks are often overconfident [232], the prevalence of defects changes with the process, and the guarantees of conformal prediction hold only while production data resembles the calibration data [237]. In addition, classical fusion rules assume independent sources [239], while the views of a defect are correlated and false alarms such as glare move with the part (Figure 32); learned fusion across views and sensors is promising [216, 230], but it is hard to validate source by source.
- **Proving low escape rates and finding unknown escapes, steps (6), (7), and (S).** Showing an escape rate below 1% with 95% confidence needs at least 299 defective parts without any escape [246, 247], and many defects are detected only with a certain probability (Figure 34) [248]. Furthermore, a running system only receives labels for the parts it rejects or sends to Review [272], so the defects it accepts remain invisible until audits or complaints reveal them (Figure 37), and active learning rarely selects defect types that look nothing like the labeled ones (Figure 40) [288]; estimating the escape rate of unknown defect types with affordable audits remains open.
- **Quality of human judgment in the loop, steps (6) and (S).** The Review class and the labels rely on people who suffer from vigilance decrement and automation bias [33, 245]. Annotators disagree on where faint defects end and on cases close to the limit (Figure 38), and agreement between annotators does not prove that the labels follow the defect catalogue, since they can share the same bias (Figure 39) [285, 287]; similarly, pre-labels from foundation models speed up the annotation [177, 178], but annotators tend to accept wrong suggestions [245]. Methods to measure and maintain the quality of human decisions without many expert labels are still largely manual.
- **Real-time guarantees and integration with the line, step (7).** Deep-learning inference on general-purpose computers and GPUs has variable processing times, and rare long delays together with queueing make decisions late at high line speeds (Figure 35); proving a worst-case latency for such systems, as a PLC does for its cycle, remains difficult. Additionally, standard information models such as OPC UA for machine vision exist [252], but many installations still rely on vendor-specific interfaces, and attributing defects to their causes across many interacting process parameters, with dead time and changing materials (Figure 36), is still largely done by hand [268].
- **Continual learning under change control, steps (7) and (S).** Models that adapt during production could follow drifts and new products, but regulators currently expect static models under change control in critical applications [277], and safe, verifiable ways to update models frequently are still an active research topic.

The table below summarizes these open problems together with the current practice that mitigates them in the meantime, as described in the corresponding steps of this document.

| **Open problem** | **Steps** | **Current practice** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Difficult surfaces and joint design | (1), (3) | Sample tests with limit samples; dome, polarization, deflectometry, or 3D sensing; enclosure and band-pass filters. |
| Consistent data over time | (2), (3) | Verification at every shift with golden parts; reference marks and patches; versioned calibration; method-noise checks. |
| High-resolution images | (3) to (5) | Tiling at full resolution; cascades that analyze only suspicious regions. |
| Robustness to new conditions | (4), (5) | New products, suppliers, and lights treated as changes that need new validation data; score and match trends monitored. |
| From anomalies to defects | (5) | Anomaly detector as a safety net beside measurement, rules, and supervised models; anomalies without a known type sent to Review. |
| Benchmarks and thresholds | (5), (6) | Thresholds set on many good images and checked on defective parts and limit samples per defect type. |
| Alignment-dependent 3D results | (4), (5) | Datum alignment for conformity decisions; best fit only to judge shape. |
| Calibrated decisions and correlated evidence | (6) | Calibrated scores rechecked after changes; fusion in part coordinates with planned overlap of the views. |
| Proving low escape rates | (6), (7), (S) | Test sets sized for the claimed rates; random audits of accepted parts; random share in every labeling batch. |
| Quality of human judgment | (6), (S) | Known test parts in the Review queue; guidelines with limit samples; golden sets and double labeling. |
| Real-time guarantees and integration | (7) | Worst-case latency measured at full load; encoder tracking; positive release; SPC before feedback control. |
| Continual learning | (7), (S) | Static models released through sandbox validation, shadow mode, versioned deployment, and rollback. |
